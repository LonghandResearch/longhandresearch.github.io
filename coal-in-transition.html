01 / Research letter

The export market
is the missing link.

Thermal coal first.
Metallurgical coal kept separate.
2026-2030 framework.

A durable fuel.
A more demanding investment case.

Coal is often discussed as a single bet on energy demand. That is too broad for an exporter. A power system can burn more coal while buying less of it from abroad, and a miner can receive a higher benchmark price while earning less on its own sales. This report therefore follows three separate questions: how much coal is consumed, how much must cross a border, and how much value remains with the producer after the shipment. The distinction matters most when a resilient global consumption headline sits alongside greater competition for the remaining import requirement.

The IEA's September update describes a near-term recovery supported by fuel substitution and weather, with demand potentially easing again in the following year. Its December 2025 medium-term framework places that cycle against expanding alternative energy supply and changing industrial uses. Those assessments answer different questions and belong to different forecast dates. We use the later update for the immediate outlook and the earlier work to frame the structural mechanisms. Neither is treated as a settled path for demand, and an energy-security response during a shock is not proof that every recent price gain will persist.

Our interpretation is that Indonesia's position should be assessed through its export opportunity rather than through global tonnage alone. The relevant buyer is the one still needing an imported cargo after domestic production, inventories and other fuels have done their work. On the producer side, the relevant advantage is the ability to deliver acceptable coal at a competitive total cost while preserving cash through weaker conditions. This suggests a more selective research process: compare the product, customer and cost structure before assigning value to an apparent national supply advantage. A large market position is a starting point for that work, not the conclusion.

The central tension is between persistence and substitution. Existing power stations and industrial processes give coal a continuing role, but that role can change in utilisation, geography and import intensity. A plant kept available for reliability need not run enough hours to support its former fuel purchases. A growing industrial market need not buy the same coal quality as a utility. We therefore treat stronger prices, tighter supply and durable demand as separate propositions to test. The interactive exhibits below make those distinctions visible. The scenario lab shows their economic consequences without pretending to predict a particular company's earnings.

02 / The demand split

The world can consume more.
Exporters can sell into less.

The global total includes domestic coal, lignite and metallurgical uses. The seaborne thermal market is a narrower pool.

The contrast is a useful starting point rather than evidence of a contradiction. Import demand is what remains when local supply and inventories are set against consumption. Under that framework, an exporter should watch the import gap as carefully as the demand total. The IEA's production assessment highlights abundant stocks and domestic supply in major consuming countries. These can limit the need for an additional foreign cargo even while coal remains an important part of the energy system. A thesis built only on rising electricity use misses that second half of the balance.

It is also important to keep thermal and metallurgical coal apart. The former is principally an energy fuel. The latter serves steelmaking requirements with different quality constraints. The older IEA trade outlook identifies stronger import prospects for metallurgical coal than for thermal coal. That does not automatically benefit a thermal producer. Product specifications, processing capability and customer access must fit the opportunity. We would therefore test any claim about "Asian coal growth" against the exact market it describes before using it in an Indonesian producer's revenue forecast.

Evidence check / Export coverage

One omitted series.
A gap still to explain.

BPS publishes coal and lignite separately. Read both before treating the atlas as Indonesia's whole export market.

The missing series materially changes the reading of Indonesia's exports. The revised BPS coal table records 390.9272 million tonnes in 2025. Its separately published lignite table records another 133.0826 million tonnes. Adding the two gives a derived customs total of 524.0098 million tonnes, compared with the IEA's preliminary thermal-coal estimate of 517 million tonnes. At the published precision, the remaining difference is 7.0098 million tonnes. The same check for 2024 gives 557.7557 million tonnes from BPS against 557 million tonnes from IEA, a difference of 0.7557 million tonnes. This is substantially more informative than comparing the coal-only table with the IEA estimate and treating the entire distance as an unexplained export shortfall.

The bridge narrows the arithmetic gap without completing a statistical reconciliation. The IEA's Coal 2025 annex distinguishes hard coal from lignite and defines thermal coal by use outside metallurgy. Customs categories and energy classifications need a verified crosswalk before their totals can be called equivalent. Different revision dates and reporting practices also remain possible explanations, rather than demonstrated causes, for the residual. We therefore retain the separate source definitions. This matters for the buyer analysis too. BPS records 131.0549 million tonnes of lignite going to China in 2025, in addition to the coal table's 81.7877 million tonnes. The atlas continues to display the coal-only series consistently, but its destination ranking and shares should not be mistaken for the complete coal-and-lignite customer mix. China cannot be assessed from that narrower denominator alone.

04 / Price formation

The benchmark has recovered.
The realised price is another question.

A dated benchmark is a market reference. It is not the revenue received by every Indonesian mine.

The price evidence points to recovery, but the basis of each observation matters. The World Bank series is a futures-price reference averaged over the selected period. The IEA separately reports late-August markers for higher-calorific Australian coal and lower-calorific Indonesian coal. These are different products and observations. We do not join them into one continuous price history or infer an Indonesian realised price from their spread. For a producer study, contract timing, calorific convention, moisture, ash, freight responsibility and customer mix would all need to be reconciled before a benchmark is translated into revenue.

The June World Bank coal discussion offers a second institutional view on substitution from gas and the pressure from domestic import replacement. It reinforces the need to separate a short-term fuel-price response from a sustained expansion in import demand. Our analytical framework asks whether the price signal survives when the original shock fades. If utilities can return to another fuel, inventories can be drawn down, or supply can restart, a higher prompt price may provide a shorter earnings window than an investment plan assumes. That possibility should be tested with several cases rather than hidden inside a single long-term price input.

05 / Indonesia's position

Supply discipline can support price.
Volume still pays the bills.

Less coal offered to the market and less coal sold by a producer are related ideas, with different consequences.

The question for an exporter

Does the price gain compensate for the tonnes lost?

Revenue combines realised price and sales volume. Cash contribution adds the product's operating cost and revenue-linked charges. A stronger benchmark alone does not settle either result.

Test the trade-off ↓

Indonesia's January policy announcement explicitly sought to align production with demand and improve market conditions. We treat it as evidence of policy intent, not as proof of the quotas currently applying to every company or the supply actually removed. Announced limits, approved plans, mine production and export cargoes need separate records. This report does not carry forward the early announcement's inconsistent volume arithmetic. Before making a stronger claim about implementation, the national and company records would need to reconcile on dates, definitions and the distinction between domestic allocation and export availability.

Our economic interpretation is conditional. A reduction in available supply can be supportive if buyers still need those tonnes and competitors cannot replace them cheaply. But a producer surrendering volume does not receive all of the wider price benefit, and the benefit can be uneven across coal qualities. The useful comparison is the cash generated by the revised mix of price, volume and cost. For policymakers, the same exercise should extend to domestic energy security and public revenue. For investors, it should extend to mine life, sustaining capital and cash retained after distributions. Those extensions require evidence beyond the simple shipment arithmetic shown here.

Company evidence / January-June 2026

The same commodity.
Different cash economics.

Two issuer cases test the mechanism. They do not represent the whole Indonesian industry or establish a valuation ranking.

Bayan Resources / Company presentation

Better prices, with the same reported sales volume

Bayan's second-quarter investor update provides a direct test of the price-volume argument. Reported sales were 32.2 million tonnes in both first-half periods. Average selling price rose from USD50.3 per tonne in H1 2025 to USD54.0 in H1 2026, while average cash cost increased from USD34.2 to USD35.0. The derived difference widened from USD16.1 to USD19.0 per tonne. Revenue increased from USD1,621.9 million to USD1,740.1 million, and reported EBITDA rose from USD533.3 million to USD627.6 million. At the presentation's rounding precision, this is an example of stronger earnings alongside unchanged sales volume. It supports the importance of realised price and cost control, without establishing how much of the improvement came from the benchmark, contract timing or product mix.

The cost definition is central to that conclusion. Bayan explicitly includes royalty, barging and selling, general and administrative expenses in its average cash cost. Entering USD35 into the scenario lab as production cost and then adding logistics and a revenue-linked charge would therefore count some deductions twice. The presentation also reports USD41.1 million of actual capital expenditure in Q1 and USD36.0 million in Q2, giving a derived H1 total of USD77.1 million. Management attributes part of the lower-than-budget second-quarter spending to project delays. That makes expenditure timing a question for subsequent quarters, rather than proof of a permanently lower capital requirement. These observations establish a better operating spread in this period. They do not establish free cash flow, sustainable dividends or the price an investor should pay for the business.

Bukit Asam / Reviewed interim accounts, unaudited

Follow the earnings through to cash

Bukit Asam offers a different exposure to the same sector. Its company-hosted operating update reports H1 2026 sales of 21.10 million tonnes, split between 10.77 million tonnes domestically and 10.33 million tonnes for export. Production was 19.45 million tonnes. The roughly 51% domestic share uses sales as its denominator, whereas the company's roughly 55% DMO statement uses production. They are different ratios. The mix makes domestic customer requirements and logistics important alongside international prices. It also illustrates why a producer's shipment result cannot be inferred from the national export forecast. The operational update is a reproduction of management statements on the issuer's website. It is useful company evidence, with the same incentive to present performance favourably as any management communication.

The interim accounts make the earnings mechanism more visible. H1 revenue rose from Rp20.452 trillion to Rp22.028 trillion, while cost of revenue declined from Rp18.205 trillion to Rp17.781 trillion. Gross margin consequently increased from approximately 11.0% to 19.3%, calculated from the unrounded statement values. The cost notes show lower mining-service and coal-transportation expenses alongside higher fuel and lubricant expense. That is a mixed cost picture, rather than evidence that every operating input became cheaper. Cost of revenue also includes royalties, depreciation and inventory movements, so it is not comparable with Bayan's average cash cost. Profit attributable to owners rose from Rp0.833 trillion to Rp2.650 trillion. Total revenue includes non-coal activities, and sales quantities come from a separate operating disclosure, so this report does not divide those totals to manufacture a management-reported selling price.

Cash gives the stronger test of the period's improvement. Net operating cash increased from Rp2.224 trillion to Rp4.626 trillion. Cash purchases of fixed assets and bearer plants were Rp1.147 trillion in H1 2026, compared with Rp1.531 trillion a year earlier. Subtracting these purchases leaves a derived Rp3.479 trillion, against Rp0.693 trillion in H1 2025. The H1 2026 statement separately records Rp0.249 trillion of lease-liability payments and Rp0.148 trillion of reclamation and mine-closure deposits, among other flows. Operating cash already reflects the statement's treatment of taxes, royalties and interest, while investment and financing commitments extend beyond the selected asset purchases. The result therefore provides evidence of improved cash conversion for one half-year. A sustainable distribution or valuation case still requires working-capital analysis, the full investment programme, debt commitments and performance across weaker price periods.

The domestic transition / PLN plan

Capacity additions are a plan.
Fuel consumption depends on execution.

The 2025-2034 announcement assigns approximately 76% of planned additions to new and renewable energy plus storage. That is a share of new capacity, not today's generation mix. Storage itself is not a primary fuel source.

For coal demand, the operational questions are which projects connect, how much energy they deliver and which generation they displace. A national capacity headline does not identify the utilisation of a particular coal station or the fuel procurement of a captive industrial plant.

The model's strength is that its accounting is visible. Sales volume multiplied by realised price gives gross revenue. The assumed revenue-linked charge and volume-dependent production and inland costs are then deducted to obtain cash contribution. The result deliberately stops there. It does not capture fixed overhead, sustaining capital, rehabilitation spending, income tax, working capital or financing, so it should not be read as distributable cash. Its purpose is to expose how the inputs interact before a more detailed company model is built.

The two issuer cases demonstrate why producers exposed to the same commodity deserve separate assessments. Bayan's reported selling-price and cash-cost spread improved with unchanged rounded sales volume. Bukit Asam's interim statements show stronger operating cash alongside lower asset purchases, with a substantial domestic sales component in its operational disclosure. Those are actual period observations, rather than settings for this hypothetical portfolio. Their cost definitions, currencies, customer mixes and financial metrics differ. A fuller company model would preserve each issuer's accounting and reconcile working capital, capital commitments and financing before valuing the cash left for shareholders. The scenario lab remains useful for testing the price-volume trade-off, provided costs and charges are entered once. It does not turn the two company examples into a sector-wide conclusion or a stock rating.

07 / Outlook & conditions for revision

What could change
our view.

Revisit the framework when the import gap, supply response or producer economics changes. Avoid relying on one headline.

01

Import requirement

Compare consumption with domestic supply, inventories and recorded imports in the same period. Reassess if Asian buyers need consistently more foreign coal despite local supply expansion.

02

Supply response

Reconcile announced limits with company approvals, actual output and shipments. Reassess if lost Indonesian availability is restored or competitors replace it faster than assumed.

03

Cash conversion

Compare realised selling prices and costs with the benchmark. Reassess if production costs, inland logistics or capital needs absorb the apparent improvement in price.

2026-2030 / Conditional research cases

Separate the recovery from the longer horizon

The evidence supports a two-speed framework. For 2026-2027, the September IEA update provides the current near-term volume forecasts used in this report, including a conditional easing of global demand in 2027. For the rest of the decade, the December 2025 IEA outlook remains an older structural reference. It forecast global consumption in 2030 approximately 3% below its own 2025 baseline, with stronger competition from alternative power sources and LNG. We do not apply that percentage to the newer demand figures or draw an invented annual path between them. The practical question is which mechanisms remain in place after the current price recovery. The three cases below describe conditions and consequences. They are author interpretations, with no assigned probabilities, coal-price targets or numerical company earnings forecasts.

Base framework / Continued use, contested imports

Resilience does not remove competition

Our base framework assumes coal remains important to existing power and industrial systems while alternative generation, gas availability and domestic mining compete for incremental demand. That combination can leave global consumption resilient without providing the same resilience to foreign cargoes. Indonesian exporters would compete on the specifications their customers need and the delivered cost of supplying them. In this case, an improved prompt price may help current earnings, but investment commitments must survive periods when import demand weakens or cost inflation catches up. The company evidence makes the test concrete. Monitor whether Bayan preserves the realised unit spread as prices change, and whether Bukit Asam sustains operating cash after its capital programme and other commitments. The case gains support from stable coal use alongside constrained imports and profitable, selective shipments. It weakens if imported volumes rise persistently despite adequate local output and inventories.

Upside for exporters / A wider import gap

Buyers need more foreign tonnes

The upside case requires more than strong electricity demand. Local supply must fail to cover the additional requirement, inventories must provide insufficient relief, or alternative fuels and generation must become less available or less competitive. Slower grid integration, expensive gas or weaker domestic mine output are possible mechanisms, not predictions in this report. Under those conditions, Indonesian availability could become more valuable and stronger realised prices could coincide with healthy shipment volumes. Producers with suitable coal and dependable logistics might benefit, provided fuel, stripping, transport and capital costs do not absorb the gain. Confirmation would require recorded imports, customer procurement and generation data aligned with domestic production and inventory changes across successive periods. A brief weather disruption or one higher benchmark observation would be insufficient. Faster replacement supply, replenished stocks or weaker purchasing would challenge this case even if global coal use remains high.

Downside for exporters / Import replacement wins

Coal is used, but fewer cargoes are needed

The downside case combines stronger alternative generation or cheaper gas with adequate domestic coal supply and inventories in the principal importing markets. The resulting pressure falls on the import requirement, which can decline faster than total coal consumption. Export competition would then matter as much as Indonesia's own supply restraint. A national cut would not guarantee a lasting price benefit if other suppliers replace the tonnes or buyers no longer require them. At company level, weaker selling prices would need to be assessed against the full cost and capital base. A favourable half-year spread would provide limited comfort if it reversed while development spending continued. Confirmation would come from sustained import declines, narrowing realised operating spreads and weaker operating cash after required investment. The case would lose support if inventories tightened, replacement supply disappointed or customers maintained foreign purchases despite a more favourable domestic supply balance.

Our conclusion / Selectivity matters

Indonesia's coal opportunity is best understood as a sequence of three tests. First, determine which foreign buyers still need imported coal after local production and inventories. Second, establish whether Indonesian suppliers can serve that requirement with the right quality, dependable logistics and competitive cost. Third, follow the revenue into cash after the mine's operating and investment commitments. The BPS lignite evidence expands the customer picture and explains why the coal-only atlas is incomplete. It does not resolve the statistical crosswalk to IEA. The company evidence supports the cash mechanism more directly. Bayan shows that earnings can improve without a rise in rounded sales volume, and Bukit Asam shows improved cash conversion in its reported half-year. Neither observation proves a durable sector recovery. The stronger conclusion is that continued coal use creates opportunities for particular businesses, with returns determined by their position in the import market and their ability to retain cash.

For investors, the next decision should begin with the durability of realised margins and cash commitments, rather than a higher benchmark alone. Review product mix, royalty and freight treatment, working-capital movements, sustaining versus development expenditure and debt or lease obligations before comparing companies. Bayan's inclusive cash-cost definition and Bukit Asam's accounting cost of revenue require separate models. Their reported half-year improvement is evidence to investigate, rather than a justified valuation premium or a buy recommendation. For policymakers, the relevant trade-off includes domestic fuel reliability, realised export receipts, public revenue and investment in the transition. Production approvals are an instrument whose effect must be measured through output, domestic deliveries and export availability. The PLN capacity plan should likewise be judged through commissioned projects and displaced generation. Announcements alone do not settle either trade-off.

We would become more constructive if matched trade balances showed a sustained need for Indonesian cargoes and subsequent issuer disclosures preserved margins and cash after investment. We would become more cautious if import substitution strengthened, competitors restored supply or operating and capital requirements consumed the apparent price benefit. The critical unresolved evidence is specific: the BPS-to-IEA classification bridge, realised implementation of production approvals and company cash performance beyond the current reporting period. These are conditions for revising the assessment, rather than reasons to dismiss the observations already available. Keeping them visible lets the research deepen as evidence arrives without turning an uncertain market story into a confident forecast.
