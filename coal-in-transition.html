01 / Research letter

The export market
is the missing link.

Thermal coal first.
Metallurgical coal kept separate.
2026-2030 framework.

A durable fuel.
A more demanding investment case.

Coal is often discussed as a single bet on energy demand. That is too broad for an exporter. A power system can burn more coal while buying less of it from abroad, and a miner can receive a higher benchmark price while earning less on its own sales. This report therefore follows three separate questions: how much coal is consumed, how much must cross a border, and how much value remains with the producer after the shipment. The distinction matters most when a resilient global consumption headline sits alongside greater competition for the remaining import requirement.

The IEA's September update describes a near-term recovery supported by fuel substitution and weather, with demand potentially easing again in the following year. Its December 2025 medium-term framework places that cycle against expanding alternative energy supply and changing industrial uses. Those assessments answer different questions and belong to different forecast dates. We use the later update for the immediate outlook and the earlier work to frame the structural mechanisms. Neither is treated as a settled path for demand, and an energy-security response during a shock is not proof that every recent price gain will persist.

Our interpretation is that Indonesia's position should be assessed through its export opportunity rather than through global tonnage alone. The relevant buyer is the one still needing an imported cargo after domestic production, inventories and other fuels have done their work. On the producer side, the relevant advantage is the ability to deliver acceptable coal at a competitive total cost while preserving cash through weaker conditions. This suggests a more selective research process: compare the product, customer and cost structure before assigning value to an apparent national supply advantage. A large market position is a starting point for that work, not the conclusion.

The central tension is between persistence and substitution. Existing power stations and industrial processes give coal a continuing role, but that role can change in utilisation, geography and import intensity. A plant kept available for reliability need not run enough hours to support its former fuel purchases. A growing industrial market need not buy the same coal quality as a utility. We therefore treat stronger prices, tighter supply and durable demand as separate propositions to test. The interactive exhibits below make those distinctions visible; the scenario lab shows their economic consequences without pretending to predict a particular company's earnings.

02 / The demand split

The world can consume more.
Exporters can sell into less.

The global total includes domestic coal, lignite and metallurgical uses. The seaborne thermal market is a narrower pool.

The contrast is a useful starting point rather than evidence of a contradiction. Import demand is what remains when local supply and inventories are set against consumption. Under that framework, an exporter should watch the import gap as carefully as the demand total. The IEA's production assessment highlights abundant stocks and domestic supply in major consuming countries. These can limit the need for an additional foreign cargo even while coal remains an important part of the energy system. A thesis built only on rising electricity use misses that second half of the balance.

It is also important to keep thermal and metallurgical coal apart. The former is principally an energy fuel; the latter serves steelmaking requirements with different quality constraints. The older IEA trade outlook identifies stronger import prospects for metallurgical coal than for thermal coal. That does not automatically benefit a thermal producer. Product specifications, processing capability and customer access must fit the opportunity. We would therefore test any claim about "Asian coal growth" against the exact market it describes before using it in an Indonesian producer's revenue forecast.

04 / Price formation

The benchmark has recovered.
The realised price is another question.

A dated benchmark is a market reference. It is not the revenue received by every Indonesian mine.

The price evidence points to recovery, but the basis of each observation matters. The World Bank series is a futures-price reference averaged over the selected period. The IEA separately reports late-August markers for higher-calorific Australian coal and lower-calorific Indonesian coal. These are different products and observations. We do not join them into one continuous price history or infer an Indonesian realised price from their spread. For a producer study, contract timing, calorific convention, moisture, ash, freight responsibility and customer mix would all need to be reconciled before a benchmark is translated into revenue.

The June World Bank coal discussion offers a second institutional view on substitution from gas and the pressure from domestic import replacement. It reinforces the need to separate a short-term fuel-price response from a sustained expansion in import demand. Our analytical framework asks whether the price signal survives when the original shock fades. If utilities can return to another fuel, inventories can be drawn down, or supply can restart, a higher prompt price may provide a shorter earnings window than an investment plan assumes. That possibility should be tested with several cases rather than hidden inside a single long-term price input.

05 / Indonesia's position

Supply discipline can support price.
Volume still pays the bills.

Less coal offered to the market and less coal sold by a producer are related ideas, with different consequences.

The question for an exporter

Does the price gain compensate for the tonnes lost?

Revenue combines realised price and sales volume. Cash contribution adds the product's operating cost and revenue-linked charges. A stronger benchmark alone does not settle either result.

Test the trade-off ↓

Indonesia's January policy announcement explicitly sought to align production with demand and improve market conditions. We treat it as evidence of policy intent, not as proof of the quotas currently applying to every company or the supply actually removed. Announced limits, approved plans, mine production and export cargoes need separate records. This report does not carry forward the early announcement's inconsistent volume arithmetic. Before making a stronger claim about implementation, the national and company records would need to reconcile on dates, definitions and the distinction between domestic allocation and export availability.

Our economic interpretation is conditional. A reduction in available supply can be supportive if buyers still need those tonnes and competitors cannot replace them cheaply. But a producer surrendering volume does not receive all of the wider price benefit, and the benefit can be uneven across coal qualities. The useful comparison is the cash generated by the revised mix of price, volume and cost. For policymakers, the same exercise should extend to domestic energy security and public revenue. For investors, it should extend to mine life, sustaining capital and cash retained after distributions. Those extensions require evidence beyond the simple shipment arithmetic shown here.

The domestic transition / PLN plan

Capacity additions are a plan.
Fuel consumption depends on execution.

The 2025-2034 announcement assigns approximately 76% of planned additions to new and renewable energy plus storage. That is a share of new capacity, not today's generation mix. Storage itself is not a primary fuel source.

For coal demand, the operational questions are which projects connect, how much energy they deliver and which generation they displace. A national capacity headline does not identify the utilisation of a particular coal station or the fuel procurement of a captive industrial plant.

The model's strength is that its accounting is visible. Sales volume multiplied by realised price gives gross revenue. The assumed revenue-linked charge and volume-dependent production and inland costs are then deducted to obtain cash contribution. The result deliberately stops there. It does not capture fixed overhead, sustaining capital, rehabilitation spending, income tax, working capital or financing, so it should not be read as distributable cash. Its purpose is to expose how the inputs interact before a more detailed company model is built.

Two producers exposed to the same benchmark can therefore deserve different assessments. One may have a lower production cost but a longer inland route; another may sell a higher-quality product while carrying greater capital needs. The next stage of research should use company disclosures to replace the illustrative assumptions and examine those differences. Until that work is complete, the scenario lab is a way to ask better questions, not a basis for a stock rating. A price recovery becomes an investment case only after the realised cash economics and the price paid for the asset have both been tested.

07 / Conditions for revision

The thesis needs
observable checkpoints.

Revisit the framework when the import gap, supply response or producer economics changes. Avoid relying on one headline.

01

Import requirement

Compare consumption with domestic supply, inventories and recorded imports in the same period. Reassess if Asian buyers need consistently more foreign coal despite local supply expansion.

02

Supply response

Reconcile announced limits with company approvals, actual output and shipments. Reassess if lost Indonesian availability is restored or competitors replace it faster than assumed.

03

Cash conversion

Compare realised selling prices and costs with the benchmark. Reassess if production costs, inland logistics or capital needs absorb the apparent improvement in price.

Confidence is higher in the distinction between consumption and import demand than in any single price path. The former is a balance that can be tested; the latter depends on changing fuel economics, weather, supply and policy. This report consequently argues for a conditional view of Indonesia's coal opportunity. Continued coal use is compatible with a more competitive export market, and a tighter market is compatible with weak cash conversion at an individual mine. Evidence of sustained import growth and stronger company-level cash generation would improve that view. Evidence of effective import substitution, replacement supply or rising costs would weaken it.
