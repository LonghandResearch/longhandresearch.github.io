# Indonesia's AI infrastructure needs a power and water plan

## Operating requirements shape the investment case

An Indonesian data center investment needs a credible route from announced capacity to usable power, dependable cooling and customers. A campus headline describes the scale of a plan. It leaves open how much capacity will be commissioned, how heavily the servers will run and whether local utilities can support the resulting demand.

That assumes {{base_load_pct}}% average electrical utilization and PUE of {{base_pue}}. It is a calculation under stated assumptions. Public evidence does not establish today's national metered electricity or water consumption for Indonesian data centers, or the share attributable to AI.

## Capacity observations have different coverage

The dated market baseline is {{baseline_mw}} MW of operational IT capacity in Greater Jakarta in the second half of 2025. Applying the current fleet base assumptions produces an estimated {{current_twh}} TWh of annual electricity. The capacity observation and the calculated electricity are different kinds of evidence. Neither is an October 2026 national consumption measurement.

The separate operator inventory identifies {{inventory_mw}} MW of disclosed operational IT capacity. Its coverage is incomplete, and many facilities overlap the metropolitan market estimate. Adding those totals would count the same market twice. Cloud region openings, investment commitments and buildings described as AI ready do not supply the missing IT capacity or operating load.

## Electricity costs depend on actual loading

In the additional 1 GW base case, annual electricity energy charges reach Rp{{electricity_cost_tn}} trillion. The calculation uses Rp{{electricity_tariff}} per kWh, the verified third quarter 2026 PLN I-4 high voltage energy rate. A qualifying industrial contract is assumed. This is a dated tariff observation, not confirmation of a fourth quarter rate or a private data center contract.

Electrical utilization matters because installed capacity can remain partly idle. The base case draws an average facility load of {{average_load_gw}} GW, while demand at full IT load is {{full_load_gw}} GW. Those figures pose different questions for annual spending and connection design. The energy charge also excludes contract premiums, minimum bills, taxes, reactive charges and backup fuel.

The annual electricity equals {{national_share_pct}}% of Indonesia's reported 2025 national consumption of {{national_twh}} TWh. That comparison helps establish scale. It does not tell an operator whether a particular substation can provide the necessary power, meet redundancy requirements or deliver it on schedule.

## A small water charge can conceal a supply constraint

With consumptive WUE of {{base_wue}} liters per IT kWh, the same base case consumes {{water_million_m3}} million m³ of direct cooling water annually. Valued at the Jakarta marginal price proxy of Rp{{water_tariff}} per m³, that consumption amounts to Rp{{water_cost_bn}} billion a year. The water cost is much smaller than the electricity cost in this scenario.

The proxy is not a purchased water bill. Utilities normally meter delivered or withdrawn water, some of which may return through blowdown or other discharge. Actual intake can exceed consumption. Jakarta's published industrial tariff also does not establish the price or supply contract for a campus in Batam, Cikarang or Karawang.

Annual averages do not establish dependable supply through the dry season. A site needs a reliable allocation, suitable treatment and permission to draw and discharge water. Those requirements need local evidence even when the modeled annual charge appears manageable.

## Cooling choices connect the two resources

PUE captures total facility electricity relative to IT electricity. It includes electrical losses and other overhead as well as cooling. WUE in this model measures consumptive water per IT kWh. Changing PUE alone therefore changes modeled electricity without automatically changing direct cooling water.

Liquid cooling transports heat from the servers into a coolant loop. The outdoor heat rejection system determines whether the site then consumes water through evaporation. A closed loop can still work with wet cooling towers. Dry heat rejection can avoid tower evaporation while requiring more fan or compressor electricity. External technical benchmarks inform these tradeoffs. They are not Indonesian operating measurements. The low PUE and zero WUE combination is an exploratory bound, not a validated Indonesian engineering design.

## Infrastructure decisions remain local

The national electricity comparison cannot establish available headroom in Java, Batam or Bintan. Investment diligence needs the local transformer and transmission plan, connection queue, backup arrangements and the meaning of each contracted feed. Redundant MVA should not be added together and presented as usable IT MW.

Water diligence needs the corresponding utility allocation, source reliability, intake and discharge permissions, treatment capacity and reuse arrangements. The repository does not include a local load flow or reservoir yield study. Its scenarios identify requirements to investigate rather than certify available power or water.

## Pipeline realization determines demand

The outlook starts from a documented subset containing 331 MW operating, 413 MW under construction and 2,424 MW planned. It combines Greater Jakarta evidence with selected Surabaya, Batam and Bintan disclosures from different dates. It remains a partial national capacity bridge.

The aggressive case activates that subset by 2030. Conservative and base cases use explicit realization assumptions through 2032. For an analyst, the useful next evidence is commissioned IT capacity, actual electrical loading, measured cooling performance and signed utility terms. Those observations would turn a broad infrastructure scenario into a more specific assessment of an operator's costs and ability to execute.
